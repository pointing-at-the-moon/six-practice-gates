## 7.7 Requesting the Turning of the Dharma-Wheel: The Entreaty That Almost Never Happened

The sixth vow, requesting the turning of the dharma-wheel.

This vow has a wellspring far back in the Āgamas — an entreaty, and one that "almost never happened." Go back first to that scene.

### Beneath the Tree of Awakening, a Thought of Silence

The *Ekottarika Āgama* has a chapter named for it: the "Chapter on Entreaty." Its opening sūtra records what happened just after the awakening:

> At one time the Buddha was in Magadha, beneath the tree of the site of awakening. The World-Honored One, not long after attaining the Way, had this thought: "This profound Dharma I have now realized is hard to understand, hard to fathom, hard to come to know. … If I teach this wondrous Dharma and people neither believe nor receive it, nor practice it, the labor will be vain and the loss real. Better that I now keep silence — why should I teach at all?"

The World-Honored One, newly awakened, sitting beneath the tree, and a thought arises: the Dharma I have realized is too deep, hard to understand, hard to fathom; taught to those who will not believe or practice, the effort is wasted. Better silence — why teach?

Feel the peril of this moment: the Buddha has awakened, the Dharma is in the world — and the Dharma comes within a breath of stopping inside that one mind, never to come out.

At that moment Brahmā, knowing from afar the Tathāgata's thought, "as swiftly as a strong man bends and stretches his arm," vanished from the Brahmā heaven, appeared before the Buddha, bowed his head to the Buddha's feet, and pleaded with all his heart. He offered that most beautiful of figures: beings are like lotuses in a pond, "risen from the soil but not yet above the water" — some still beneath the surface, some about to break it, some already standing clear, unwetted by the water; if these beings whose roots are ripening "perish without hearing the Dharma, is it not a grief? Now is the very time — may the World-Honored One teach the Dharma!"

The World-Honored One, knowing Brahmā's thought and out of tender care for all beings, then spoke a verse:

> Brahmā has come now to entreat: the Tathāgata opens the Dharma's gate; those who hear shall gain firm faith, discerning the Dharma's profound essentials.

From this, the wheel of Dharma turned. So it was: that this world ever heard the Buddha's Dharma hung, at the first, upon one entreaty. To request the turning of the dharma-wheel is no polite formula of liturgy; it is to take Brahmā's ancient plea onto one's own shoulders.

### Samantabhadra Spreads That One Plea Across the Dharma-Realm

Samantabhadra's sixth vow is the carrying of Brahmā's plea up to the Flower Ornament's scale:

> Further, good son! To request the turning of the dharma-wheel is this: throughout the realm of dharma and the realm of empty space, in every dust-mote of all buddha-fields of the ten directions and three times, each holding vast buddha-fields beyond speaking, in each land, moment by moment, Buddhas beyond speaking attain perfect awakening, encircled by ocean-like assemblies of bodhisattvas — and I, with the karma of body, speech, and mind, by every kind of skillful means, earnestly entreat them all to turn the wondrous dharma-wheel.

Look at those words — "moment by moment." In every dust-mote of the whole dharma-realm, in moment after moment, Buddhas beyond measure are just now attaining perfect awakening. The Buddhas are awakening moment by moment, and so I am entreating moment by moment: before every newly awakened Buddha "I" am present, with body, speech, and mind, earnestly pleading — teach the Dharma. The verse sings:

> All the lamps of the world in the ten directions, the first to accomplish awakening — I now entreat and invite them all to turn the unsurpassed, wondrous dharma-wheel.

Every lamp of the world in the ten directions — those Buddhas just lit, the first to accomplish awakening — I now entreat them all: turn the unsurpassed, wondrous dharma-wheel. Every newly awakened Buddha is a lamp just kindled; to entreat is to go and carry each lamp before the eyes of beings.

### This Vow Is Not in the Heavens; It Is Beside You

Samantabhadra spreads requesting the dharma-wheel across the dharma-realm, and it sounds like an affair of the heavens. But brought down onto an ordinary person, it lands very near.

Beside you, whoever is willing to speak the Dharma to you — a Dharma teacher ascending the seat to give instruction, a good friend urging you to recite the Buddha's name, a sūtra-scroll set open before you — that is the "dharma-wheel" turning one round here, with you. Requesting the turning of the dharma-wheel, brought down into daily life, is this: to treasure every chance to hear the Dharma, to be willing to open one's mouth and ask of a good teacher, to support the places where the Dharma is still spoken and beings are still led in reciting the Buddha's name. The Dharma will not turn to your ear of itself; it has reached this day only because generation after generation of people were willing to request, willing to speak, willing to protect it. That you can hear one line of "Namo Amitābha Buddha" today is because, for two thousand five hundred years, countless people requested it in relay and turned it in relay. Now it is your turn — will you, too, turn this wheel one more round for those who come after?

### This Vow and Buddha-Recollection Are One Thing

Requesting the turning of the dharma-wheel and buddha-recollection look like two affairs; in fact they guard the same thing.

The Name you recite — "Namo Amitābha Buddha," "Namo Original Teacher Śākyamuni Buddha" — what is it? It is the Dharma the Buddha realized, condensed into a single line. The thousand sūtras and ten thousand treatises that the turning wheel rolls out are, in the end, all gathered back into this one Name; reciting this one line, you are receiving the fruit of that entreaty, that turning of the wheel.

So this vow of requesting the dharma-wheel, brought down onto one who recites the Buddha, is especially intimate: to ask that the Dharma abide in the world, and to hold the Name thought after thought, is to tend the same lamp. One end asks the Buddha to speak the Dharma out; the other end recollects the spoken Dharma back into the heart. And this vow closes, again, on those eight words: "moment by moment continuous, without interruption; my actions of body, speech, and mind without weariness." Entreating moment by moment is just like holding the Name moment by moment — one thought joining the next, asking that lamp to stay lit forever.

The Buddha is willing to teach. But the teaching done, the Buddha will yet show His entry into extinction. The next vow, requesting the Buddhas to abide: asking the Buddha, and every good teacher — do not go; stay a while longer.
